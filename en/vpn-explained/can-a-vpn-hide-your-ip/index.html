VPN explained

Can a VPN Hide Your IP Address?

Hiding your IP address is the one thing a VPN does unambiguously: the site you visit sees the server's address instead of yours. What that buys you depends entirely on who was supposed to see it.

Which IP address we mean

Your public IP address is the one your internet provider assigned to your connection — the string a site uses to see roughly where you are and who carries your traffic. The private address inside your home network never leaves it anyway.

When you connect to a VPN, requests leave from the server, so the destination records the server's address. Your own address still exists and is still bound to your line; it simply is not in the request any more.

What the site sees instead

It sees an address belonging to a datacenter, in a country you chose, shared with everyone else using that server at that moment. From the destination's point of view the visitor became a crowd — which is exactly how streaming sites detect and block VPN traffic, by matching addresses against lists they maintain.

That shared crowd is also the protection: nothing about you is in the address itself, and the same server carries thousands of unrelated people's traffic.

Detection is straightforward rather than clever. Providers of blocking services publish lists of addresses belonging to hosting companies, geolocation databases record which ranges are datacenters rather than homes, and a site checks the visitor against both. Nothing about you is involved — the address alone is enough to raise a flag.

Who still knows your real address

The VPN provider, which accepted your connection from it, knows your account and your address together unless its no-logs claim has been audited. Your internet provider still sees your address and sees that you are connected to one VPN server, even though the destinations are hidden.

Sites you are signed into do not need the IP address at all — your login is a stronger identifier. And anyone able to watch both ends of a connection — the same public wifi, an employer, a state network — can match timing and volume even when addresses and content are encrypted.

Shared address or dedicated address

Most VPN plans give you a shared address, which is the default for privacy: your traffic blends into everyone else's. A few providers sell a dedicated address as an add-on — one address reserved for you.

That trades privacy for reputation. A dedicated address stops looking like a VPN to sites that block datacenter ranges, which helps with banking and stubborn logins, and gives anyone who records it a stable string to associate with you. Use one only where you need it, and keep the shared address for everything else.

There is also an IPv6 detail worth knowing. If your connection supports IPv6 and the tunnel only carries IPv4, requests can escape over the other protocol and reveal your real address. Modern clients avoid this by disabling IPv6 outside the tunnel or carrying it inside — worth checking once on your own setup.

What a hidden IP is actually good for

It removes the address-based part of your exposure: sites no longer see your provider or your city, your internet provider no longer sees the destinations, and your public address stops appearing in things like the torrent swarm, where every participant sees it — Best VPN for torrenting.

It does not remove who you are when you sign in, and it does not resist someone correlating traffic patterns. If your goal is a changed address, a VPN does that job cleanly; if your goal is anonymity, the address was never the hard part: our guide to VPN anonymity.

What the address does and does not say

A VPN server's address says where the server is and nothing about who is behind it. That is the whole benefit: the visitor's city, country and internet provider are replaced by a datacenter that thousands of other people share.

It is also the limit. Geo-location itself becomes approximate, so services that rely on precision — local delivery, taxi pickups, fraud checks that expect a home address — will treat you as a visitor from somewhere else, and sometimes that is exactly what you wanted.

Your own address does not change at all. It stays bound to your line, and it reappears in every request the moment the tunnel drops. A hidden address is a property of an up connection, not of your account.

Frequently asked questions

Does a VPN change my IP address permanently?

No — it changes it while the tunnel is up, and your original address returns the moment you disconnect. Your provider still assigns the same address to your line, and the VPN server's address is the same for everyone who uses it.

Can someone find my real IP address with a VPN on?

Not from the traffic you send through the tunnel. They can find it from somewhere else: a site you are signed into, a file you share directly, a WebRTC leak in a misconfigured browser, or an account that has your address on file. The tunnel hides the address on the wire, not the data you hand over.

Do websites know I use a VPN?

Usually yes. The server's address sits in published ranges and databases, so a site can tell that the visitor arrives from a datacenter shared by many people. That is why some sites block it and why some services quietly accept it — knowing and caring are different things.
